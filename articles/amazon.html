Amazon — Is Logistics Becoming the Real Moat?

Did Amazon accidentally build a logistics company while trying to build the world's best online store—and can that infrastructure become a standalone growth engine?

The strategic problem Amazon solved

Amazon's original customer promise was selection, price and convenience. But selection without reliable delivery is not a durable proposition. The company therefore built fulfillment centers, transportation, last-mile capabilities, inventory systems and increasingly sophisticated demand forecasting.

This created a physical network that is difficult to replicate. The key insight is that e-commerce economics are not purely digital: the customer ultimately cares about the physical movement of a product.

From cost center to moat

Amazon's logistics network can reduce delivery time, improve inventory availability and support Prime. These benefits reinforce customer frequency, which generates more data and volume, which improves network utilization.

This is a classic flywheel: more customers → more volume → denser network → lower cost and faster delivery → better customer proposition → more customers.

The new strategic move

In 2026, Amazon announced that its supply-chain infrastructure would be available to businesses across sales channels. This is strategically significant. Amazon is effectively turning an internal capability into an external platform.

The move resembles AWS: Amazon first built infrastructure for itself, learned to operate it at scale, then monetized the capability for third parties.

The competitive threat

Walmart, Shopify, regional logistics players and specialized fulfillment providers can attack different parts of the chain. Meanwhile, AI may improve competitors' inventory planning and route optimization.

The deeper threat is margin dilution. If Amazon opens its logistics network broadly, it must balance external customers with its own retail needs. If service levels decline, Amazon damages the retail flywheel that made the network valuable.

What Amazon should do

Amazon should prioritize external logistics customers where their volume improves network density without creating operational conflict with Prime. It should avoid becoming a commodity parcel carrier.

The differentiation should be end-to-end orchestration: inventory placement, fulfillment, transportation, returns and demand prediction. The product is not a truck; it is a lower total cost-to-serve.

The company should also use logistics data to create new services for sellers—dynamic inventory placement, automated replenishment, cross-border optimization and working-capital tools. This increases switching costs and expands monetization beyond delivery fees.

CEO takeaway

Amazon's most defensible asset may be the physical infrastructure beneath its digital interface. The strategic opportunity is to monetize that infrastructure without commoditizing it. The lesson is broader: capabilities built as internal solutions can become powerful external platforms when they have accumulated enough scale, data and operational learning.
