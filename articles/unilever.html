Unilever — Can a Smaller Portfolio Produce Faster Growth?

Can reducing complexity create more growth than adding more brands and categories?

The conglomerate problem

Large consumer companies often accumulate businesses because each acquisition looks attractive in isolation. Over time, however, management attention fragments, supply chains become complex and capital is allocated across businesses with very different growth and return profiles.

Unilever's recent strategy is a useful response to that problem. The company has emphasized fewer priorities, stronger brands, productivity and portfolio optimization, while its Foods business has been combined with McCormick following the demerger process.

Why simplification can be a growth strategy

Simplification is often described as cost cutting. The more interesting interpretation is strategic focus.

If a company has limited management capacity, capital and marketing investment, allocating those resources to fewer categories can increase the probability that each brand becomes distinctive. A simpler organization can also make decisions faster and improve accountability.

Where Unilever can win

Unilever's strongest positions are in categories where brand, science, distribution and consumer insight reinforce one another. Beauty and wellbeing, personal care and home care can benefit from premiumization, innovation and culturally relevant marketing.

The company has also been investing in digital and creator-led marketing. Its 2026 news flow highlights creator-led initiatives and an AI-powered supply-chain agenda, illustrating a broader shift: marketing and operations are becoming increasingly data-rich and dynamic.

The competitive threat

The threat is not only Procter & Gamble or private label. It is fragmentation. Digital channels lower the cost of launching niche brands, while consumers can discover products through creators without traditional mass-media investment.

Unilever therefore needs to defend scale advantages while behaving like a portfolio of faster-moving challengers.

The better portfolio model

Unilever should classify businesses into three buckets: defend-and-scale, build-and-test, and exit-or-partner.

Defend-and-scale brands receive disproportionate marketing and innovation investment. Build-and-test categories receive small, rapid experiments with clear kill criteria. Exit businesses should be sold or partnered rather than consuming managerial attention.

The critical discipline is to measure not just growth but incremental return on brand investment. A €1 million marketing increase should have an explicit hypothesis: penetration, frequency, premiumization or new occasion. This creates a more analytical portfolio than simply asking which brand is "strategic."

CEO takeaway

The strategic lesson is counterintuitive: a company can grow faster by owning less. Portfolio simplification works when it releases scarce managerial resources toward businesses with a real right to win. The risk is becoming so focused that the company loses future options. The answer is not maximum breadth or maximum focus, but an explicit portfolio logic for where to experiment and where to concentrate.
